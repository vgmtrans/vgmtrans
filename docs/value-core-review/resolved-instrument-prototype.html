The flow

Consider an attack on instrument 5 with a changed envelope, followed by a program change to instrument 7 while the first voice continues. The emitter gives both segments the same voice identity. Preparation chooses a variant for that voice. The next fresh attack starts another voice using instrument 7. Later passes use these decisions directly.

Executed events + private bank copies
    ↓ preparePerformance
Walk events in order:
    remap track-local voice IDs → resolve/adapt each voice once
    ↓ assign output addresses after all variants exist
ResolvedPerformance: segments + voices + banks + final addresses
    ├─ renderMidiSequence(prepared)
    └─ selectSynthBanks(prepared) → SF2 / DLS

InstrumentHandle is a bank index and an instrument index. It remains valid when preparation appends a variant and reallocates an instrument vector. Source identities and preferred addresses are retained on the original instruments. A generated variant starts with neither: preparation assigns its output address after all variants exist.

Preparation records the original instruments' preferred numeric addresses before creating variants. This also distinguishes an original instrument with an implicit bank/program zero from an unassigned variant. Later source lookups cannot accidentally select a generated variant.

For stitching, each distinct collection is prepared once and receives a compact, disjoint bank range. Repeated occurrences share the frozen instruments and reuse their completed performance and addresses. MIDI is then rendered with those final addresses. Output filtering selects instruments without erasing handle targets.

Entry point: ResolvedPerformance.h. Implementation: ResolvedPerformance.cpp. Integration: CollectionBinding.cpp, Export.cpp, and CollectionStitch.cpp.

Choices and resolved inputs describe different things

Collection::selection records the original choices. An automatic collection retains its sequence and supplemental inspection assets. A manual collection also retains the user's ordered banks and sample pools, including selected pools that no bank uses.

Collection::inputs.banks records the resolved banks. Each entry contains a bank ID and its own sample uses, with their placement settings, resolution status, and alternatives. For example:

Manual choices
  sequence S, bank X, pools [A, B]

Resolved inputs
  bank X
    pool A starting at sample 4
    pool A starting at sample 12

Pool B remains a user choice and is available for collection export. Bank X receives only its two uses of A. members() computes the flat membership view, retaining explicit choice order and adding automatic uses once each. Automatic resolution does not fill a second stored list of audio members.

The resolver validates provider types and manual candidate restrictions. These IDs are stored with the assets in the same immutable session snapshot. Preparation visits each bank entry and reads its sample uses directly; it still checks snapshot lookups, prepared bank identity, instruments, and sample references. A failed selection blocks preparation regardless of diagnostic severity. Incomplete or ambiguous selections can retain usable fallbacks.

Each bank first prepares its samples through its own format callback. The sequence can then configure the private bank contents and its playback runtime together. SequencePreparationContext::banks<Data>(format) exposes only matching banks, in selected order, with the data retained by bank preparation. Scanned assets and previous prepared collections remain unchanged.

SegSat reserves exact native bank matches before assigning unmatched logical numbers. The same callback applies those logical numbers to instrument identities and velocity data, and gives a lone bank preferred output bank zero. There is no separate bank-assignment callback or stored mapping handoff. Bank-count warnings occur during preparation; standalone banks keep their native mapping.

Discovered collections are resolved again as assets change, retaining their sequence-based identity. User collections retain their chosen resolution rather than automatically rematching when new files arrive. Supplemental inspection assets keep their own status and do not block audio preparation merely because an inspection reference is missing.

Representation: CollectionModel.h. Resolution: AssetResolution.cpp. Preparation: CollectionBinding.cpp. Format API: AssetResolution.md.

Ownership and API

preparePerformance takes ownership of its event and bank values. It is the only public construction path for ResolvedPerformance. The result exposes const access to both, together with its assigned addresses. Consumers cannot replace its events with unresolved selections, remove an instrument behind a handle, or mutate the banks after address planning.

The frozen bank vector is held by a shared_ptr<const …>. Copies of a prepared result share that immutable bank allocation while retaining their associated voice and address records. MIDI conversion borrows the prepared result for the duration of the call. It returns an independent MidiSequence; temporary track events never escape the conversion API.

Source and prepared events still use the same structures. Preparation assigns each note a voice ID indexing an owned SoundingVoice record. It consumes the optional source instrument override into that record, then clears the per-note override. Prepared segments refer to one authoritative selection through their voice. The result exposes the segments, voices, and banks through const access.

Completed collection preparation

PreparedCollection consumes a BoundCollection and the requested preparation options. Its constructor executes the sequence when requested, applies sequence modulation, resolves instruments and variants, assigns addresses, and freezes the banks before returning. The binding's runtime and mutable bank storage are then discarded. There is no public render() step or render-once convention.

The result always retains one immutable bank allocation and the snapshot owning its external sample-pool metadata. When rendering succeeds, its ResolvedPerformance shares that same bank allocation. Two shared ownership handles refer to the same data; banks are not copied between the two objects. soundBanks() always reads that allocation, and performance() reports whether there is a resolved performance.

Preparation outcomeCompleted result
Sequence execution not requestedBanks and sample inputs are available; no performance is invented
Sequence execution failsBanks and sample inputs remain available, with rendering diagnostics and no performance
Sequence execution succeedsResolved events share the final banks; original events remain available for inspection

This preserves bank-only and WAV exports as well as paired output. Encoded sample bytes still belong to SourceStore; retaining sample metadata does not change that lifetime contract. Stitching retains a shared completed result for each distinct collection, so repeated occurrences share its events, banks, modulation data, and sample owners instead of copying or extracting them independently.

The result replaces CollectionWorkspace. The implementation removes its mutating render method, the fallback between two bank owners, and the separate sample/performance/modulation fields in StitchPart. Its replacement cost is a preparation-options value, completed collection metadata, and an additional shared ownership handle for the bank allocation. Relative to checkpoint 0c24c8f42, this stage is neutral in physical and noncomment production lines; the improvement is the simpler lifetime contract, not a source-size reduction.

One completed input for output conversion

An instrument selection and its output address answer different questions: which definition plays, and where the destination can find it. Both answers now belong to ResolvedPerformance. There is no separate public InstrumentAddressPlan to construct, mutate, or pass alongside the performance.

auto prepared = preparePerformance(events, banks, {
    .dynamicEnvelopes = true,
    .onlyUsedInstruments = true,
});
auto midi = renderMidiSequence(prepared);
auto synthBanks = selectSynthBanks(prepared);

InstrumentPreparationOptions sets adaptation, used-only selection, and the optional first bank for a stitched part. The resolver makes variants first and assigns addresses last, within the same preparation call. PreparedCollection forwards these options through its instruments field. Stitching supplies the next available bank when constructing each distinct part, then reuses that completed result for repeated occurrences.

Output code calls prepared.selectionFor(note) or prepared.selectionFor(programChange). The returned ResolvedInstrument contains an instrument pointer and its final bank/program address. An external preset has a null pointer and a usable address. MIDI no longer dereferences the note's optional source-selection field or interprets its variant. Synth selection uses the same pointer/address record.

Used-only preparation still assigns addresses to program changes with no notes, because MIDI must emit them; synth selection collects instrument handles from sounding voices. The initial instrument can supply pitch-bend context even when it needs no output slot. Address exhaustion sets valid() to false and adds an error to the performance's diagnostics. MIDI rendering returns those diagnostics without tracks, and synth selection returns no banks. Prepared bank data remain available for inspection.

This narrows the consumer interface without creating a second set of event types. Source and prepared events still share their structures; prepared notes identify an owned voice instead of carrying duplicate resolved instrument selections. selectionFor follows that voice inside the result. Callers must use notes and handles from that result. The interface does not make arbitrary cross-performance event mixing impossible at compile time.

The simplification is the removal of a separately managed plan, a second diagnostics list, repeated plan arguments, and independently supplied used-only policies. Its replacement cost is preparation options and three selection accessors. Address allocation itself remains necessary. Choosing a different bank namespace now happens before preparation; there is no mutable replanning step.

A note segment belongs to a sounding voice

A driver can change pitch while the same instrument continues sounding. A PerformanceVoiceId identifies that sounding lifetime; a PerformanceNoteId identifies the note that a pitch motion addresses. Several note IDs can belong to one voice.

A format calls note() for an attack and continueVoice(previousNote, event) to keep a named predecessor's voice and lane. Continuing at the realized pitch reuses the note ID; changing key creates another note ID on that voice. The returned note ID is the anchor for subsequent pitch motion. A gate-only edit uses setNoteEnd().

auto attack = out.note(NotePerformanceEvent{.key = 60, .durationTicks = 8});
auto next = out.at(8).continueVoice(
    attack, NotePerformanceEvent{.key = 64, .durationTicks = 8});

// Optional: reach the new key gradually instead of immediately.
out.at(8).pitchSlide(next, 60, 64, 4);

For a source tie, set event.extendsPrevious = true and call continueVoice() with the predecessor. This explicitly keeps its note ID and existing pitch motion, even when the event repeats a glide target that has not yet been reached. The driver decides what counts as a tie and which envelope or LFO phases restart.

The emitter assigns note and voice IDs; fresh attacks do not copy those IDs from event parameters. An invalid or unknown predecessor starts a fresh voice. The older note() tie shorthand remains: with extendsPrevious, it extends event.note when supplied, otherwise the latest emitted note. An explicit predecessor through continueVoice() identifies the intended voice when notes overlap.

The two notes share their instrument and hardware deadline. The optional slide changes the pitch path. Stopping, replacing, or making that slide immediate leaves voice ownership unchanged. A new attack can also have a slide; the presence of pitch motion does not imply continuation.

TickSource actionVoice
0Attack instrument 5 at key 60A
2Attack instrument 7 at key 72 on another laneB
4Extend the first note at key 60A
8Continue the first voice at key 64A

Source note and voice IDs are local to a track. Preparation maps each source voice to a distinct entry in the completed performance. Its first segment creates a SoundingVoice containing the resolved instrument and optional absolute hardware stop tick. Later segments use that same record. Gate timing stays on the segments. Manually constructed source events without a voice ID each start a fresh voice; shared note IDs alone do not declare shared ownership.

The hardware limit is calculated from source segment times using the song's tempo map. An initial timer and any later shortening contribute their earliest deadline. A voice limited to 1,000 milliseconds stops at tick 14 if ticks last 50 milliseconds through tick 8 and 100 milliseconds thereafter. Splitting it into portamento notes cannot restart that timer. The overlapping voice B keeps its own deadline and instrument.

MIDI note planning reads each voice’s deadline and resolves every physical gate before encoding. It follows the last planned attack of each voice to incorporate later extensions. The renderer receives those completed gates and emits each note once.

MIDI conversion follows the segments of each explicit voice to find the preceding physical note and its bend reference key. An immediate key change needs no source pitch automation: lowering supplies its destination transition when no boundary slide is present. It uses the preceding motion's realized pitch and honors the same pitch-bend/portamento policy as explicit slides. Temporary MIDI segments retain the prepared voice ID.

Envelope and individual LFO restart flags remain independent. extendsPrevious describes extending a physical note; portamento can emit another physical note with the same voice ID. Source command headers and playback spans remain available for highlighting.

The source timeline uses NotePerformanceEvent. MIDI lowering produces temporary MidiNoteEvent values for its current track. MIDI notes retain the source boundary’s metadata and restart flags, and add a physical gate end, bend reference, and output action. Control-event definitions are shared through PerformanceEventWithNote; lowering returns no source sequence or prepared-performance object.

Source identity: PerformanceEmitter.cpp. Prepared records: ResolvedPerformance.h. Physical note output: PerformanceMidiRenderer.cpp.

MIDI conversion works on one track at a time

renderMidiSequence borrows a completed ResolvedPerformance. It collects the song's tempo, transpose, meter, masked reverb changes, and level headroom from that immutable input. It then converts tracks in order:

  1. detail::lowerMidiTrackEvents reads one track's source events and pitch automations. It plans physical notes and portamento controls, resolves gates and hardware deadlines, then generates pitch bends using the planned attack boundaries. Its note spans borrow their source notes; temporary MIDI notes and retained controls are owned by the resulting event vector.
  2. The renderer combines those events with the song-wide reverb changes and writes MIDI channel messages. Voice IDs still refer to the original prepared result for instrument selection. Note existence and gate ends have already been decided.
  3. The temporary events and their pointer timeline are destroyed before converting the next track. The accumulated MIDI output owns its messages.

Only the current track needs a temporary event vector. The voice table, instrument addresses, banks, source automations, and playback spans remain in the prepared input. There is no second ResolvedPerformance, and output code has no privileged access to its internals. The public conversion call remains renderMidiSequence(prepared).

Reading shared controls from the whole source song matters: a reverb or transpose command in a later track can affect an earlier track. Headroom is also a song-wide decision. Pitch lowering changes notes and pitch controls without changing the pan gains used for that calculation. Lowering warnings are appended to the output's diagnostics; source diagnostics stay unchanged, and channel-encoding warnings follow the lowering warnings.

Decide which MIDI notes exist before encoding them

A source note boundary and a MIDI Note On serve different purposes. A tie can lengthen an existing note while resetting tremolo at the tie’s tick. A changed key can bend that same note, or start another physical note through native portamento. Those operations can all belong to one sounding source voice.

MidiNoteEvent retains the boundary’s source association and independent restart flags. For an attack, its endTick is the completed physical gate end; the inherited durationTicks retains the source boundary’s duration. Its bendBaseKey identifies the key against which pitch offsets are measured. The completed event has one action:

ActionRendering behavior
AttackEmit one Note On with the completed gate duration. A fresh zero-duration attack is retained.
ContinueThe preceding attack already includes this extension. Apply the boundary’s LFO behavior without emitting another note.
ExpiredThe source voice’s hardware deadline has passed. Preserve the timeline boundary for controller flushing, but emit no note or note-triggered reset.

For example, key 60 at tick 0 with a four-tick gate, followed by a four-tick tie at tick 4, produces an attack whose gate ends at tick 8. The tick-4 boundary remains available to reset an individual LFO. If a continuation changes to key 64 through pitch bend, its reference remains key 60. If native portamento starts a physical key-64 note instead, subsequent continuations use key 64 as their reference.

Ordinary source notes and native-portamento fragments use the same MIDI note record. Each source note span begins with one segment. Portamento can retarget it, split it, or extend its overlap with a destination. When native portamento replaces a span, conversion emits those segments; otherwise it retains the original source boundaries and their individual restart decisions. Logical source gate ends remain available for curve sampling.

planPhysicalNotes walks the ordered boundaries, applies the prepared voice deadline, and combines extensions into their preceding planned attack. A continuation request with no preceding physical note becomes an attack. Bend-reset scheduling and range planning both use these completed actions. A continuation after a hardware deadline therefore cannot start a new range interval or prematurely reset the preceding curve’s release-tail pitch.

The renderer applies instrument and controller changes, honors independent LFO resets, and encodes the planned attacks. It keeps no map of previously emitted notes and never edits an earlier MIDI note’s duration. Encoding saturates durations at the MIDI model’s 32-bit limit.

Pitch placement and current pitch

Most slides specify their pitch directly: sample the declared curve and subtract the MIDI note’s reference key. Their placement needs no bend-history query. Two cases inspect the pitch already in effect:

  • A held transition checks whether its declared start matches the held pitch. That decides whether to inherit the live pitch or establish the declared start.
  • A delayed slide starting away from the note’s key checks whether earlier writes have already established that starting pitch. Otherwise it inserts the initial offset at note onset.

For a key-64 note with a slide from 60 to 64 beginning at tick 4, conversion may insert −4 semitones at note onset. But if a source wheel already establishes key 60 before tick 4, that earlier insertion is omitted. Any intervening source writes keep their original order. A delayed slide that starts at the note’s own key needs no such insertion.

SourcePitchTimeline records source wheel and range changes together. It retains the normalized wheel so a query uses the source/instrument range active at the queried command. Native portamento reads the wheel and its current range from the same source-state snapshot. Generated transitions use the same range context when interpreting a normalized source bend.

Pitch conversion keeps source writes and generated transition samples in one temporary list. Before resolving it, the converter checks the event order and sorts only when needed. Sorting is stable: when a curve produces two samples at a note boundary with the same tick and execution order, the later sample uses the new physical note's key.

The resolver keeps the primary bend, a held-note offset, and the transitions that own those layers. A source wheel retains its normalized position so it can be interpreted using the range active at the queried time. A held-note transition contributes a semitone offset. Source writes, held transitions, absolute transitions, and resets each follow an explicit branch; unrelated pitch layers pass through unchanged.

A scheduled reset applies only while its transition still owns the layer. A later source write therefore survives an old transition's reset. Reset scheduling uses the same affected notes as curve sampling, including a note that attacks exactly when a continuing curve ends. Necessary pitch queries and output emission use the resolver's resulting state. Reset selection takes the first eligible attack from the ordered physical-note timeline.

A delayed slide can add a starting-pitch write back at the note's onset. The temporary write list retains that placement, while subsequent source writes keep their own order. The immutable source performance remains unchanged.

Choose a bend range before the attack

A MIDI pitch wheel is a fraction of the channel's configured bend range. If a note will bend upward by six semitones, conversion reserves enough range when that note attacks. This lets the subsequent wheel values describe the whole motion.

planPitchBendRanges examines the events from each physical MIDI attack to the next, carrying source pitch state through those intervals. A tie stays in the current interval; simultaneous attacks share the channel's range. A new physical note produced for native portamento starts another interval. The last interval includes the events after note-off, preserving release-tail pitch.

For example, an attack at tick 0, a tie at tick 2, and a six-semitone bend at tick 3 reserve six semitones at tick 0. If a fresh attack at tick 6 needs only the source's current five-semitone range, conversion restores that source sensitivity there. Instrument requirements and simulated vibrato still contribute to the final MIDI sensitivity.

The planner records a range change against the first event at the attack's tick and execution order. Controls with that exact same position receive the change before being rendered, even when they appear before the note. The renderer uses the attached decision directly. Source-range changes remain ordinary events; they are not copied into a second change timeline.

Internal track lowering: PitchTransitionMidiLowering.h. Rendering and shared controls: PerformanceMidiRenderer.cpp.

Keep selected instruments with their bank

Synth conversion previously received a list of banks and a separate optional list of instrument/address pairs. It built a pointer-to-address map and walked the banks to recover their membership. Exporting one bank also searched each remaining bank to determine which instrument pointers belonged to it.

SynthExportInput now owns one list of SynthBankSelection values. Each contains a bank reference and its selected instrument/address entries. Conversion visits those entries directly; selecting one bank removes whole groups. The same input is reused for SF2 and DLS.

SynthExportInput
    soundBanks
        bank A → selected instrument + output address
                 selected instrument + output address
        bank B → no selected instruments
    samplePools → external sample owners

The two selectSynthBanks overloads construct that list explicitly. Standalone export selects all instruments at their source address preferences. Performance export follows the addresses and used-only policy already stored in the completed performance. An empty selection always means no instruments; conversion has no absent-selection mode that silently selects an entire bank.

The bank reference has two remaining jobs. It supplies local samples, and it determines a common region-sampling step across the entire bank, including unselected instruments. Banks with empty selections remain available for unfiltered sample export. External sample pools retain their existing order and filtering rules. These are required inputs, not membership information to reconstruct.

The selection lists are owned by SynthExportInput, so a builder's temporary return value cannot leave a dangling selection span. Instrument and bank data remain borrowed from the prepared collection or standalone bank, which must outlive conversion. Sample-reference gathering reads the entries directly and no longer creates an intermediate list of instrument pointers.

The replacement cost is one bank-group record and its instrument list, plus an explicit standalone selection builder. It removes the optional flat selection, the pointer/address map, bank-membership searches, the pointer-only projection, and the per-conversion copy of the flat selection. See SynthExportData.h and SynthExportData.cpp.

Output policy

  • Selection: exact native identity first, then numeric-address fallback. The first matching definition in selected bank order wins. Fallbacks and conflicts are diagnosed once per source selection. Used-only export retains the same winner.
  • Missing instruments: notes retain external preset addresses, allowing standalone MIDI without bank data. Those addresses reserve output slots and participate in stitched bank planning.
  • Address planning: preserve preferred addresses where possible; allocate unused portable addresses for variants and conflicting definitions. Exhaustion marks preparation invalid before paired output is written.
  • MIDI without a companion: retain original instruments and report envelope events that would require private SF2/DLS variants. Do not emit references to those absent variants.
  • Failed MIDI rendering: a surviving full-bank export retains native synth modulation. The requested MIDI simulation policy only replaces it when a performance was actually prepared.

These policies retain the prototype's deliberate changes to ambiguous selection and address exhaustion. Ordinary exports still preserve historical bank-number conventions; the limitations below remain relevant.

Remaining work

Collection input relationships, the preparation lifecycle, instrument handoffs, resolved output contract, and explicit voice ownership are implemented. The following work remains:

  • Shared event structures. MIDI notes now have a distinct type carrying the completed physical plan. They retain the source boundary’s fields, and control-event definitions remain shared. A wholesale separation of the event vocabularies is not implemented.
  • Pitch-transition conversion. Range decisions attach directly to attack boundaries, and pitch queries read the resolver’s state. Physical notes are planned before bend resets and range intervals. Held and earlier-start queries still replay the earlier write prefix; ordinary curve placement does not. Delayed slides can still insert pitch at an earlier note onset. Portamento splitting and curve placement remain separate operations.
  • Prepared bank identity. Input relationships and SegSat mapping are implemented. Preparation still edits copies of complete SoundBankAsset values and checks their identity afterward; separating immutable discovered identity from prepared musical contents remains a distinct possible refinement.

These are deletion targets, not implemented improvements or a promise of a particular line saving. Complete and validate each stage before implementing the next. The architecture review retains the broader design rationale and now tracks this sequence.

Release confidence also requires real-file testing: no representative corpus is configured or supplied here, and no Qt playback or listening validation has been performed.

Two policies particularly need that evidence. First, some real files may depend on multiple definitions matching one selection; the new deterministic first-match behavior must be checked against them. Second, historical logical banks above the portable range can still be projected differently by MIDI and SF2 encoding. The paired-output tests establish agreement for portable and compacted addresses; this change does not redefine every legacy bank convention.

Validation should target those cases directly. The public exporters now consume completed preparation results, but describing this as “only release validation remains” would overstate the design's completeness.
